# Cross-Sport Market Screening — project brief

**Drop this into the new project as `CLAUDE.md` or alongside it. It is
self-contained: it assumes no knowledge of the NBA project it came from.**

Take `screen_market.py` with you. It implements Stage 1 and runs on any sport.

---

## 1. Objective

Find sport × market cells where the closing line is **biased enough to beat the
vig**, and rank them by how fast that bias can be confirmed.

This is a **market-selection** project, not a modelling project. The prior
project spent months building models for one market and the single most valuable
number it produced was that moving from −110 to −105 was worth more than every
predictive feature discovered across twenty-one feature families combined.
**Where you bet dominates how well you predict.** Screen first; model only in a
market the screen says is worth modelling.

---

## 1a. The governing question

**Do not ask what variable predicts outcomes. Ask what pricing transformation
the bookmaker is likely simplifying, and where that simplification becomes
materially wrong.**

This is the organising principle for everything below, and it is earned rather
than borrowed. The prior project tested twenty-one families of "what predicts
scoring" and found nothing tradeable, because the market already knows what
predicts scoring — that is the one thing it is built to know. The market's
weakness is not in what it knows but in **how it converts what it knows into a
price**.

### A hypothesis has three clauses, not one

Naming a shortcut is the beginning, not the finding. All three must hold:

1. **The book applies a simplifying rule.** Demonstrate it — regress the price
   on the inputs a mechanical rule would use and show a high R². If R² is low,
   the price is a judgement and there is no shortcut to exploit.
2. **The rule discards something that predicts the residual.** This is where
   most candidates die, and it is the clause that gets skipped.
3. **That something is knowable before the bet.** Separately fatal, and invisible
   to every statistical control.

Three worked examples from the prior project, one per failure mode:

| candidate | clause 1 | clause 2 | clause 3 | outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| live 2H total | ✅ R²=0.78, coef 0.4925 on the pregame total — the book literally halves it | ❌ both discarded variables (blowout risk, first-half pace) are genuinely uninformative | — | **dead** |
| end-of-season overs | ✅ book raises its late-season total 0.5pt | ✅ actual scoring rises 3.2pt | ✅ it is a calendar | **live** |
| departed-star fade | ✅ | ✅ 55.9% ATS, 7 of 7 seasons | ❌ "gone for the season" is only knowable afterwards; every executable version ≤ break-even | **dead** |

**A mechanical rule that discards noise is a correct rule.** The 2H total looked
like the perfect target — a book halving its pregame number in seconds — and the
things it ignored turned out to be worth ignoring.

### Where simplifying rules live

You cannot observe a book's model, but you can observe which prices are
**derived** from other prices. Primary markets — full-game side and total — get
direct attention and sharp feedback. Everything downstream is generated by
formula: halves, quarters, team totals, alternate lines, props, and the
spread→moneyline mapping. **Screen derived markets, not primary ones.** The prior
project spent months on the single most-attended price in American sport and
found it efficient, which should have been the expected result.

### The taxonomy of shortcuts

1. **Insufficient shrinkage** — overweighting noisy recent outcomes
2. **Insufficient interaction** — generic strength instead of matchup-specific
3. **Wrong distributional assumption** — right mean, wrong variance or tails
4. **Mechanical translation** — correct primary valuation converted too simply
   into a derivative market

**Types 1–3 were tested and found arbitraged in primary NBA markets.** Type 4 is
where the remaining hope is, precisely because derivative prices inherit
simplifications from the primary price without inheriting the scrutiny.

⚠ A fifth mode, found late: **stale calibration.** A ratio that was right when
fitted and has since moved. NBA third-quarter scoring rose 5.22 points across
eight seasons and Q3 is now the *highest*-scoring quarter, having been the
second-lowest. Any quarter model calibrated on multi-year averages is now wrong
in a consistent direction. Look for distributions that have moved recently, not
only rules that were always wrong.

### When you cannot name the shortcut

Test for its **signature** instead. If a line is optimal its residual is
unpredictable from any pre-tipoff function, which licenses a battery of omnibus
tests that fire without identifying a cause: Mincer-Zarnowitz calibration,
within-team serial correlation, variance components across groupings, mixture
structure, conditional variance, sign runs. In the prior project this found three
real effects in one run, after twenty-one named-variable families had found
none. `omnibus.py` travels with this brief.

⚠ **Detection is not exploitation.** All three of those detections replicated and
none was tradeable: market bias +0.543 points, spread slope 0.9368, variance
rising with the spread. **The market's errors are real, measurable, and
consistently about half the size of the vig.** Finding structure was never the
hard part.

## 2. The decision rule

For each sport × market, from a line history:

```
resid  = actual − line          (actual = total points, or margin + spread)
b      = mean(resid)            the bias
σ      = sd(resid)              the noise
```

The market is beatable **only** if the empirical win rate of the better side
exceeds the break-even rate implied by the price:

| price | break-even | notes |
|---|---|---|
| −110 | 52.38% | standard US sides/totals |
| −105 | 51.22% | reduced juice |
| −102 | 50.50% | exchange, after commission |
| dime line (MLB/NHL ML) | ~50.5–51% | varies with favourite price |

**Rank on `edge_pp` = best_side_% − break_even_%.** Positive is a candidate;
anything ≤ 0 is dead at that price.

### ⚠ Do not rank on b/σ

An earlier version of this rule compared `b/σ` against `z_vig = Φ⁻¹(break-even)`
and called a market beatable when the ratio exceeded 1.0. **That rule is wrong
and will produce false positives.** A binary bet depends on probability mass
*near the line*, not on the mean. Fat tails — blowouts that were already going
over — inflate `b` without moving a single game across the number.

Measured on NBA totals: `b/σ ÷ z_vig` at −102 reads **2.28**, implying a
comfortable edge. The empirical best side is **50.38%** against a 50.50%
break-even — no edge at all. `screen_market.py` reports both and labels which to
believe. Keep `b/σ` as a diagnostic for cross-sport comparison; never as the
decision.

### Forecast bias is not betting bias

`b` measures how wrong the line is. `edge_pp` measures whether you can be paid
for it. These are different quantities, and a market can have a large, highly
significant amount of the first and none of the second — because **a bet pays
on which side of the line, not by how much.** An over by 25 pays exactly what
an over by 1 pays.

Both totals markets measured so far show this exactly:

| market | b | t | central 80% | tail 20% | best side | edge @ −110 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NCAAB totals 23-24 | +0.864 | **3.93** | **−0.083** | +0.947 | 50.86% | −1.52pp |
| NBA totals 8 seasons | +0.515 | **2.74** | **−0.152** | +0.667 | 50.38% | −2.00pp |

Both have unambiguous forecast bias — `t` of 3.93 and 2.74 — and in both the
central 80% leans slightly *under* while the mean says *over*. The entire
signal is blowouts running over more often than they run under. This is the
mechanism behind the `ratio` false positive, and it is why `ratio` reads 4.23
on NCAAB, worse than the 2.28 it reads on the NBA.

### ⚠ Do not build a second gate out of this

The obvious move is to form `C = tail_contribution / total_bias` and reject
cells with C near 1. **That statistic was built and tested here, and it does
not work.** Three measured failures:

- **No shape-independent threshold.** A pure level shift — the fully
  payoff-relevant case — gives C = 0.64 on a normal residual but **C = 1.16 on
  the actual CBB residual shape**. On a skewed residual, 1.16 is what a genuine
  edge looks like, so "C near 1 is dead" would reject a real one.
- **The denominator explodes.** On de-biased CBB data (mean ≈ 0) C ran to
  **1.8 × 10¹⁵**. Every low-bias market — i.e. most of them — returns garbage.
- **It does not separate.** Genuine +1.0 central shift → C = 1.16. The same
  bias loaded entirely into a 2% tail → C = 1.56. CBB as observed → C = 1.10.
  Too tight to threshold.

A tail-inflation measure (normal-predicted over-rate minus actual) fails the
same way: the de-biased CBB baseline already shows **+1.77pp** of inflation
from skew alone, before any bias exists.

**`edge_pp` already measures what these proxy for, and measures it correctly.**
Report the central/tail split to *understand* a cell; never to accept or reject
one. One gate, not two.

### How many points do you actually need?

`pp_per_point()` gives the exchange rate between a bias in points and a bias in
win rate, measured locally rather than assumed normal:

| market | pp per point | points of **central** bias to beat −110 | to beat −105 |
|---|---|---|---|
| NCAAB totals | 2.43 | **0.98** | 0.50 |
| NCAAB sides | 4.07 | 0.58 | 0.30 |
| NBA totals | 1.72 | 1.39 | 0.71 |
| NBA sides | 2.58 | 0.92 | 0.47 |

Two things fall out. **Sides need roughly half the points totals do**, because
σ is smaller, so a point of spread is worth far more win rate than a point of
total — worth remembering before assuming totals are the softer market. And the
targets are large: about **a full point of genuine central bias** to beat −110.
No cell measured in either project has more than a tenth of that. Compare
`central b` against `pts needed` directly; `screen_market.py` reports both.

### Detection time is a hard constraint

Games needed to detect a bias of the observed size at 80% power:

```
n_required = 7.84 / (b/σ)²
```

Because both terms scale with the sport, this is sport-independent: **a bias
exactly at the −110 break-even size needs ~2,200 games to establish.** Half that
size needs ~8,800.

| sport | games/season | seasons to confirm a break-even-size bias |
|---|---|---|
| **College basketball** (D-I) | ~5,500 | **0.4** |
| **MLB** | 2,430 | **0.9** |
| Soccer (top-5 European) | ~1,750 | 1.3 |
| NHL | 1,312 | 1.7 |
| NBA | 1,230 | 1.8 |
| College football | ~800 | 2.8 |
| NFL | 272 | **8.1** |

**This must be read against decay.** In the prior project a genuine +3.0-point
bias in mismatch games ran for six seasons and then vanished. A market needing
eight seasons to confirm an edge cannot confirm one before it dies. **NFL is
structurally unscreenable for marginal edges** despite being the most popular
market.

Note the scope of that claim: the table above fixes the bias at break-even size.
*If* an NFL market carried twice that bias it would confirm in 2.0 seasons, and
at three times, 0.9. No such bias has been measured — that conditional is
algebra, not a finding, and NFL derivatives have not been screened here or in
the prior project. What the arithmetic does establish is narrower: NFL cannot
confirm an edge that only just clears the vig, and that is where most real edges
live. Read "unscreenable" as "only worth screening for effects big enough to be
obvious," not as "excluded" — and not as a hint that such effects are there.

## 3. What makes a sport attractive

In priority order.

**1. Volume.** Fix this first. It is the lever you can verify before acquiring
any data — you know a sport's games/season in advance, whereas you only learn
its bias by screening it. Detection time is what decides whether an edge is
confirmable before it decays, and volume is the half of it you can choose. This
puts college basketball first. (It is not the *only* lever on detection time —
see "Volume and asymmetry are not independent levers" below.)

**2. Price.** The vig is the denominator and it varies more across sports than
bias does. MLB and NHL moneylines quote dime lines — roughly 2% hold against
4.5% for −110 — which cuts the required edge by a factor of 4–5. Screen every
market at the price **you can actually get**, not at −110 by convention.

**3. Attention asymmetry.** Bias survives where modelling attention is thin.
Look for: lower divisions, women's competitions, secondary markets (first half,
team totals, alternate lines), early-season games, and leagues where the book is
pricing off a generic model rather than a bespoke one.

**4. Low σ relative to bias.** Helpful, but do not screen on it directly — it is
already inside `edge_pp`. Note the ambiguity that caused confusion in the prior
project: *irreducible outcome variance* being high is bad for you (it dilutes any
bias), while *market model error* being high is good (it is the bias). They are
different quantities that both get called "low accuracy."

**5. Data availability.** A market you cannot get five years of closing lines for
cannot be screened, however attractive in theory.

### Volume and asymmetry are not independent levers

It is tempting to say volume is the only thing that shortens detection time. It
is not, and the difference changes where you look.

`n_required = 7.84 / (b/σ)²` does not contain volume at all. Volume converts a
fixed `n_required` into calendar time — a **linear** effect. Attention asymmetry
raises `b/σ` itself, and because the term is squared, it cuts `n_required`
**quadratically**. A market with twice the bias needs a quarter of the games.

Seasons to confirm, as a multiple of break-even-size bias:

| sport | games/season | 1.0× | 1.5× | 2.0× | 3.0× |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| College basketball | ~5,500 | 0.4 | 0.2 | 0.1 | 0.0 |
| MLB | 2,430 | 0.9 | 0.4 | 0.2 | 0.1 |
| Soccer (top-5) | 1,752 | 1.3 | 0.6 | 0.3 | 0.1 |
| NHL | 1,312 | 1.7 | 0.7 | 0.4 | 0.2 |
| NBA | 1,230 | 1.8 | 0.8 | 0.4 | 0.2 |
| College football | ~800 | 2.8 | 1.2 | 0.7 | 0.3 |
| NFL | 272 | 8.1 | 3.6 | 2.0 | 0.9 |

(games required: 2,198 / 977 / 549 / 244)

⚠ **Provenance — no sport in this table has been screened.** The games/season
column is arithmetic on league structure (NFL 32×17/2 = 272; NBA and NHL
30/32×82/2; MLB 30×162/2; CFB ~134 FBS×12/2; NCAAB ~360 D-I×~31/2 ≈ 5,580;
top-5 soccer 380×3 + 306×2 = 1,752). The body of the table is the
`n_required` formula divided by that column. **The 1.5×–3.0× columns are
hypothetical** — they show what detection time *would* be at those bias sizes,
not a measured `b/σ` for any of these sports. Nothing here is evidence that any
sport carries a bias above break-even size; establishing that is the entire
job of Stage 1. Treat this table as a planning aid for where to spend screening
effort, never as a result.

The two levers **multiply**, so the ordering above is about which to fix first,
not which is worth more. The best cell in the screen is the intersection: a
high-volume sport's *thin* markets — NCAAB first halves, team totals, alternate
lines, low-major conferences — where volume supplies the sample and asymmetry
supplies the bias. Screening a high-volume sport's headline market optimises
one lever and forfeits the other.

**Practical consequence:** rank Stage 1 candidates on `seasons to detect`, which
`screen_market.py` already computes from the *observed* `b/σ` per cell. That
number combines both levers correctly. Do not rank on games/season, which is
only the numerator of one of them.

### The structural tension

**Bias and liquidity are inversely related.** Markets with surviving
inefficiency are thin — low limits, wide prices, and your own bet moves them.
Markets deep enough to absorb real money have been modelled by everyone. The
screen will surface edges you cannot fund. Record the **limit** alongside the
edge, and treat `edge_pp × available_limit` as the real ranking, not `edge_pp`.

## 4. Traps that will fool you

Every one of these cost real time in the prior project.

**Pooled figures hide decay.** Always plot bias **by season**. The mismatch edge
was six good seasons then two dead ones; pooled over ten years it looks robust.
A candidate is only interesting if the by-season plot is stable *and the recent
seasons are still positive*.

**Search methods manufacture significance.** A depth-3 regression tree on 29
features found a leaf with a +2.27 point bias on 2,160 games at **t = 5.86**
(p < 1e-8). Refitting the same tree on 400 *shuffled* targets produced a larger
leaf bias 62% of the time — the observed result sat at the **38th percentile of
its own null**. Out of sample the strongest leaves reversed, correlation −0.46.
**Any search-based method needs a permutation null built in from the start.** A
tree's best leaf is an extreme order statistic, not a hypothesis test.

**Multiplicity across cells.** 20 sports × 3 markets = 60 tests; ~3 clear
p < 0.05 on noise. Use Benjamini-Hochberg across the whole screen. In the prior
project, testing 30 teams individually produced exactly 1 nominal hit against
~1.5 expected — and 0 survived correction.

**Rare conditions are untestable.** A filter firing on 0.3% of games yields ~4
games/season; eight seasons gave n=29 and a confidence interval 36 points wide.
Prefer conditions that fire on **5–10%** of games. The one real edge found fired
on ~11%.

**Predicting line movement is not profit.** The prior project could predict the
direction of the open-to-close move ~60–70% of the time and earned +1.9 points
of CLV — with **no win rate**, because the move being predicted *is the market
correcting itself*. Front-running a correction that happens anyway produces CLV
and no money. **Screen against the CLOSE.** If you screen against the opener,
you are measuring how beatable the opener is, which is a different and much less
valuable question.

**Confidence ranking can fail even when direction works.** In the prior project,
development win rate rose monotonically with model confidence (50.0% → 55.4%),
while in holdout it peaked early and *reversed* (54.4% → 50.1%). Direction calls
survived out of sample; the ranking did not. If you build a model, validate the
ranking separately from the direction.

**Executability is a separate test, and no statistic detects it.** The
departed-star signal passed significance, permutation, and 7-of-7 season
consistency, and was worthless: it keyed on "the star never played again", which
is only knowable in hindsight. For every candidate, write the execution steps
before believing the number — what you would know, and when. Every variable must
be re-tested using only information available at bet time.

**Persistence is a cheap pre-filter.** Before modelling anything, check whether
the candidate effect predicts *itself* from one period to the next. Team-level
line movement looked real cross-sectionally (95th percentile of its permutation
null) but had a year-over-year correlation of **−0.013**, swinging from −0.44 to
+0.30. If an effect cannot predict itself, it cannot predict anything. This test
costs one line of code and would have killed several ideas faster than modelling
did.

## 5. Protocol

**Stage 0 — acquire.** For each sport, get ≥5 seasons of: date, teams, closing
line (total and spread), final score. That is the whole requirement.

**Stage 1 — screen.** Run `screen_market.py` on every sport × market × price.
Output the ranked table plus a by-season bias plot for each cell. Cheap, fast,
no modelling. Most cells die here.

**One gate: `edge_pp > 0` at a price you can obtain.** Report `central b`,
`tail b` and `pts needed` alongside it so you can see *why* a cell passed or
failed — but do not turn them into a second test (see the warning in §2).

**Stage 1b — name the transformation.** Before any conditional test, write down
which price in the cell is DERIVED and from what. Regress it on the inputs a
mechanical rule would use. A high R² identifies the rule; the residual is then
the only place an edge can live. Skip this and Stage 2 becomes angle-mining.

**Stage 2 — conditional bias.** For surviving cells only, test pre-registered
conditional filters (large spreads, extreme totals, rest, travel, scheduling
spots). Permutation-controlled. Pre-register the filter list **before** looking.

**Stage 3 — decay check.** Plot the candidate's bias by season. Reject anything
whose recent two seasons are flat, regardless of pooled significance.

**Stage 4 — holdout.** Hold out the most recent full season in every market,
untouched through Stages 1–3. Write the pass/fail criteria down before looking
at it.

**Only then** consider building a model — and only for the specific market and
condition that survived.

## 6. Data sources (verify before relying on any of these)

**Historical line archives**
- `sportsbookreviewsonline.com` — free per-season spreadsheets with open/close
  spread and total plus scores, for NBA/NFL/MLB/NHL/NCAAB. Closest to the
  required schema.
- OddsPortal — broad league coverage including lower-tier soccer.
- Covers.com, SportsDatabase (SDQL) — free query interfaces.
- Kaggle / GitHub — many scraped datasets, variable quality.

**Package-based (highest quality)**
- `nflfastR` (R) — `spread_line`, `total_line` back to 1999. Clean, but NFL's
  272 games/season makes it the worst screening target.
- `hockeyR`, `pybaseball`, Retrosheet — free scores/schedules; join lines
  separately.

**Live pricing**
- The Odds API free tier (~500 requests/month) — enough to sample current prices
  across books, which is how you populate the *price* column honestly rather
  than assuming −110.

⚠ Free sports-data sources churn. Verify each is live before planning around it.

## 7. Calibration anchors from the prior project

Use these to recognise what null and non-null look like.

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| NBA totals bias vs close | +0.515 pts on σ 18.16, t = 2.74 |
| …as a win rate | best side 50.38% vs 52.38% break-even → **no edge** |
| NBA sides bias | −0.167, t = −1.19 → **no bias at all** |
| a *real* conditional edge (since decayed) | +3.0 pts, 55.8% overs, 6 seasons |
| feature families tested, all null | 21 |
| best model R² on game totals | ~7.6% |
| value of −110 → −105 | +1.16pp win rate |
| **NCAAB totals bias vs close** (23-24, n=5,588) | **+0.864 pts on σ 16.44, t = 3.93** |
| …as a win rate | best side 50.86% vs 52.38% → **no edge** |
| …its central 80% contribution | **−0.083** (C = 1.10) |
| NCAAB sides bias | +0.038, t = 0.25 → flattest cell yet measured |
| price NCAAB totals would need | **−103.5** (95% CI: +101.8 to −109.1) |

**The market is not unbiased — it is biased by about half a point in the NBA
and nearly a full point in college basketball, repeatably and at high
significance. The vig band is wider than the bias, and the bias is in the
wrong part of the distribution.** That is the correct mental model. The
screen's question is never "is there bias" but two questions in sequence:
*is the bias bigger than the price*, and *is it in the middle or the tails*.

College basketball is the useful case to hold in mind. It is roughly twice as
biased as the NBA in sd units, exactly as the attention-asymmetry argument
predicts — and it is still unbettable, because the extra bias arrived as
blowouts rather than as games crossing the number. **Finding a more biased
sport is not the same as finding a beatable one.**

## 8. Deliverables

1. `screen_market.py` pointed at every sport acquired, with a committed ranked
   table and per-season bias plots.
2. A short `FINDINGS.md` recording every cell tested — **including the nulls**,
   with their numbers. Nulls are the majority of the value and are what stop the
   same ground being re-covered.
3. A pre-registration for any cell advancing to Stage 4, written before the
   holdout is touched.
4. An explicit recommendation: which market, at which price, with which limit,
   and what the expected edge and its confidence interval are.

## 9. Stopping rules

- No cell clears `edge_pp > 0` at an obtainable price → **report that and stop.**
  It is a legitimate and valuable result.
- A cell clears `edge_pp` only at a price you cannot get → dead. NCAAB totals
  clear at −102 and nowhere else; the market trades at −110.
- A cell clears but needs > 4 seasons to confirm → deprioritise; decay will
  likely beat you.
- A cell clears but limits are too small to matter → record it and move on.

**Do not start modelling to rescue a market the screen rejected.** That is what
the prior project did for months, and the screen would have said no in an
afternoon.
